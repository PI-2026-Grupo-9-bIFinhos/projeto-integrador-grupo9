Students: Murilo Dalla Barba Temporini and Pedro Miguel Kliemann

Diet That Does Harm

It was an ordinary day in the city of São Paulo, where a family—a father, a mother, and their 17-year-old son—went about their daily routine. The son had always relied entirely on his mother; he got everything he wanted, and whenever he asked for something, she would provide it immediately. He would often ask her to cook various things, but his mother always made sure to include foods that would support his health.

A year later, the son turned 18 and soon saw an opportunity to leave home and live on his own. His mother packed his belongings and told him, "Keep taking care of your diet." In the first few days, the son paid no mind to his mother’s advice; he spent all his money on high-calorie, non-nutritious foods and gained a significant amount of weight. Deciding to turn his life around, he went to the grocery store and bought various products labeled as "fit," "light," or "calorie-free." After a week of consuming these items, he realized he lacked energy and was beginning to fall ill. He went to his mother’s house, where she explained that such products would not improve his health—rather, what he needed were wholesome, nutrient-rich meals. He went to the market and bought the foods his mother had suggested—such as rice, beans, salad, and meat—and began taking better care of his diet, which led to weight loss and improved health.
It was then that he learned an important lesson: a healthy diet is one that includes a variety of macronutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, and fats) and micronutrients (vitamins and minerals), rather than just foods that are merely *claimed* to be good for you.
